Anytime Trajectory Prediction

Abstract

Automated vehicles require not only perception of their environment, but also prediction of the actions of surrounding agents. While this field is well studied, most existing work focuses on autonomous vehicles with high computational resources, rather than platforms such as drones, which operate under strict latency constraints and limited hardware capabilities.

To address this gap, we introduce Anytime Trajectory Prediction (ATP), a lightweight, adaptable trajectory prediction pipeline. ATP supports anytime inference, allowing it to produce usable predictions under varying constraints.

I. Introduction

Accurate trajectory prediction is a key technique in autonomous vehicle control systems. In a standard pipeline, models are given sensor data of the environment, identify obstacles, and forecast their future positions or velocities. Furthermore, downstream tasks and hardware availability impose hard constraints on the performance and size of the model. This is a complex problem that has received significant attention, as effective obstacle avoidance is the difference between a safe and unsafe autonomous navigation system.

We introduce a model that enables accurate trajectory prediction for lightweight machinery using a single RGB sensor. Our work consists of the following key features:

  • Anytime querying: We introduce an anytime query method that allows our model to meet downstream-defined latency requirements.
  • Probabilistic trajectory modeling: We model future trajectories as a mixture of Gaussians, enabling an explicit and structured representation of multimodal uncertainty.
  • Ability to reason about interactions between agents: We design the model infrastructure to allow the model to consider pairwise interactions between agents.

Anytime querying is the key feature of our model, unlike existing methods, we defined our model to be able to run for a varyable amount of iterations in order to continuously refine its predictions. Users can choose to run the model for a limited amount of iterations for low-latency but less confident predictions, or alloow the model to run for longer in order to recieve more accurate predictions.

II. Related Works

Classical approaches to trajectory prediction often rely on state estimation techniques such as Kalman filters, which assume linear dynamics and Gaussian noise, but struggle with complex, multimodal behavior. Consequently, neural networks have become widely used in the field of trajectory planning.

Additionally, with the rise of autonomous driving, a multitude of contests and datasets for object prediction have been created, including the Waymo Open Motion Dataset and related benchmarks.

This has led to many new state-of-the-art algorithms that address the challenge by outputting multimodal trajectory predictions. Specifically, our model is inspired by the following two areas of work:

Mixture Density Networks: Mixture Density Networks (MDNs) are models that output mixtures of Gaussians. These approaches have been applied to trajectory prediction and related sequence modeling tasks. However, training them has been shown to be difficult in practice due to their tendency to collapse into a single mode. As a result, several alternative training methods have been proposed, including a “best-of-N” loss function, where only the best prediction is considered. This encourages the model to predict a diverse range of trajectories, an approach that we adapt in our work.

Diffusion Models: Diffusion-based trajectory prediction methods model future trajectories through iterative denoising processes and have recently demonstrated strong performance on multimodal motion forecasting tasks. These methods provide flexible generative modeling capabilities for complex trajectory distributions.

Iterative Refinement

To enable anytime refinement in ATP, we adopt an iterative denoise-refinement algorithm. Beginning from a fully random trajectory, the model progressively refines its prediction by conditioning on scene context at each step. The algorithm works as such:

  1. Initialize a trajectory by sampling from a standard Gaussian distribution.
  2. Pass this trajectory into ATP, conditioned on scene information, to produce a refined prediction.
  3. If the refinement budget is exhausted, return the current prediction. Otherwise, corrupt the prediction with Gaussian noise according to a scheduled noise level and repeat from step 2.

The noise scheduler controls the magnitude of noise injected at each iteration. By gradually reducing this noise level across refinement steps, the model is able to incrementally improve its prediction rather than regenerating a trajectory from scratch at every step. This allows ATP to be queried at any point during refinement by trading computation for accuracy, making it well-suited for latency-sensitive deployment scenarios.

Training

ATP was trained on the Waymo Motion Dataset [1]. At each training step, the model received features extracted from the previous [N] frames, including bounding box information and scene-level context from the scene extractor.

It consists of three key components:

  1. Negative Log-Likelihood Loss — penalizes the model when the ground truth trajectory is assigned low probability under the predicted distribution.
  2. Diversity Loss — discourages mode collapse,* ensuring the model produces a varied set of plausible trajectories rather than converging to a single prediction.
  3. Smoothness Loss — enforces approximate physical plausibility by encouraging trajectories that are temporally smooth and consistent with realistic motion dynamics.

IV. Results

In this section, we display both quantitative results to benchmark the performance of our model, as well as qualitative trajectory predictions to explore strengths, biases, and areas of improvement for the model. All results are evaluated using a held out portion of the Waymo Dataset.

Qualitative

We first evaluated model performance through qualitative analysis on sample videos. Below, we visualize ATP's most likely predicted trajectory for a vehicle approaching an intersection:

Frame 1

ATM tracks the movement of an incoming car, and over 8 iterations it refines its output to predict the car will continue to go straight..

1 / 3
As demonstrated above, ATM, due to its training on the Waymo dataset, accurately predicts the likely movements of vehicles in a variety of scenarios. The example also highlights ATM's ability to incorporate new information in real time. As the car above approaches the intersection, the model initially predicts a right turn; however, once the car proceeds through the intersection without signaling, the model correctly updates its prediction to straight ahead. The first frame further illustrates ATM's anytime refinement in action. Even early refinement steps correctly predicted the vehicle going straight. Despite being relatively inaccurate, this is already useful in a time-constrained pipeline, but with additional computation, ATM's prediction converges closer to the ground truth.

Quantitaive

We additionally recorded quantitative results to numerically verify the effectiveness of ATP. Our evaluation focused on three key properties:

  1. Accuracy — How closely do our trajectory predictions match the ground truth?
  2. Mode Diversity — How distinct are the predicted trajectory modes, and does our model avoid mode collapse?
  3. Iterative Refinement Effectiveness — Does prediction accuracy improve as the model is given additional refinement iterations?
