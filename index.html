Anytime Trajectory Prediction

Abstract

Automated vehicles require not only perception of their environment, but also prediction of the actions of surrounding agents. While this field is well studied, most existing work focuses on autonomous vehicles with high computational resources, rather than platforms such as drones, which operate under strict latency constraints and limited hardware capabilities.

In this work we will present ATP, a model based on a anytime prediction framework for which the agent’s trajectories are iteratively refined, allowing the model to output a usable result after a limited amount of steps or be improved for longer if more compute is available. Our approach is based on the transformer architecture and a representation of probable trajectories as a mixture of gaussians.

This system has proven to be suitable for real-time trajectory prediction while adapting to the agent’s resources. From qualitative results, we observed that the model outputs accurate predictions even at early refinement steps, and that it can predict different probable trajectories. From quantitative results, the model achieves strong performance on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, but doesn't improve with additional refinement steps as expected. However, we provide improvement directions that we believe will allow the model to fully leverage the anytime prediction framework. Latency-wise, the model is able to output predictions in a suitable time frame for real-time applications.

I. Introduction

Accurate trajectory prediction is a key technique in autonomous vehicle control systems. In a standard pipeline, models are given sensor data of the environment, identify obstacles, and forecast their future positions or velocities. Furthermore, downstream tasks and hardware availability impose hard constraints on the performance and size of the model. This is a complex problem that has received significant attention, as effective obstacle avoidance is the difference between a safe and unsafe autonomous navigation system.

We introduce a model that enables accurate trajectory prediction for lightweight machinery using a single RGB sensor. Our work consists of the following key features:

  • Anytime querying: We introduce an anytime query method that allows our model to meet downstream-defined latency requirements.
  • Probabilistic trajectory modeling: We model future trajectories as a mixture of Gaussians, enabling an explicit and structured representation of multimodal uncertainty. This allows our model to capture multiple plausible future trajectories for a given agent.

Anytime querying is the key feature of our model, unlike existing methods, we defined our model to be able to run for a variable amount of iterations in order to continuously refine its predictions. Users can choose to run the model for a limited amount of iterations for low-latency but less confident predictions, or allow the model to run for longer in order to receive more accurate predictions.

This mechanism is particularly relevant for real time use-cases in low-capability hardware, such as embedded systems on UAVs, as the available compute budget can vary depending on the control frequency or the urgency of the navigation decision.

II. Related Works

Classical approaches to trajectory prediction often rely on state estimation techniques such as Kalman filters, which assume linear dynamics and Gaussian noise, but struggle with complex, multimodal behavior. Consequently, neural networks have become widely used in the field of trajectory planning.

Additionally, with the rise of autonomous driving, a multitude of contests and datasets for object prediction have been created, including the Waymo Open Motion Dataset and related benchmarks.

This has led to many new state-of-the-art algorithms that address the challenge by outputting multimodal trajectory predictions. Specifically, our model is inspired by the following two areas of work:

Mixture Density Networks: Mixture Density Networks (MDNs) are models that output mixtures of Gaussians. These approaches have been applied to trajectory prediction and related sequence modeling tasks. However, training them has been shown to be difficult in practice due to their tendency to collapse into a single mode. As a result, several alternative training methods have been proposed, including a “best-of-N” loss function, where only the best prediction is considered. This encourages the model to predict a diverse range of trajectories, an approach that we adapt in our work.

Diffusion Models: Diffusion-based trajectory prediction methods model future trajectories through iterative denoising processes and have recently demonstrated strong performance on multimodal motion forecasting tasks. These methods provide flexible generative modeling capabilities for complex trajectory distributions.

III. Methodology

Anytime Trajectory Model pipeline.

Our task is formally defined as follows. Given a sequence of RGB frames and a target agent, we aim to learn a distribution \( P(\tau) \) where \( \tau \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times 3} \) represents a trajectory as a sequence of \( (x, y, z) \) coordinates over \( t \) timesteps. Here, \( x \) and \( y \) denote spatial position, while \( z \) represents the log size of the agent's bounding box — used as a proxy for distance. The distribution \( P \) is modeled as a Gaussian mixture with \( N \) modes, allowing the model to capture the \( N \) most likely trajectories a given agent may follow.

Critically, inference must support variable-length computation: the model should be queryable at any point during processing, trading speed for accuracy depending on the deployment constraints. To achieve this, we build on Astra [cite] as our core architectural inspiration, and extend it with an iterative refinement process that enables continuous improvement of predicted trajectories over time.

Feature Extractor

Using YOLO26 nano for object detection and ByteTrack for tracking allows to perform both global and local feature extraction. This sequence of model and algorithm constitutes the feature extractor block in our pipeline. It is responsible for processing raw RGB inputs and extracting relevant information about the scene, such as:

  • Bounding box information for each detected object.
  • Object IDs that are consistent across frames.
  • Object class labels.
  • Confidence scores for each detection.
In addition to detection- and tracking-related features, we also use intermediate YOLO layer outputs to encode inputs into compact representation, both at the scene level and at the object level. These features can then be fed into the model for downstream trajectory prediction.

Iterative Refinement

To enable anytime refinement in ATP, we adopt an iterative denoise-refinement algorithm. Beginning from a fully random trajectory, the model progressively refines its prediction by conditioning on scene context at each step. The algorithm works as such:

  1. Initialize a trajectory by sampling from a standard Gaussian distribution.
  2. Pass this trajectory into ATP, conditioned on scene information, to produce a refined prediction.
  3. If the refinement budget is exhausted, return the current prediction. Otherwise, corrupt the prediction with Gaussian noise according to a scheduled noise level and repeat from step 2.

The noise scheduler controls the magnitude of noise injected at each iteration. By gradually reducing this noise level across refinement steps, the model is able to incrementally improve its prediction rather than regenerating a trajectory from scratch at every step. This allows ATP to be queried at any point during refinement by trading computation for accuracy, making it well-suited for latency-sensitive deployment scenarios.

Training

ATP was trained on the Waymo Motion Dataset [cite]. At each training step, the model received features extracted from the previous [N] frames, including bounding box information and scene-level context from the scene extractor.

It consists of three key components:

  1. Negative Log-Likelihood Loss — penalizes the model when the ground truth trajectory is assigned low probability under the predicted distribution.
  2. Diversity Loss — discourages mode collapse,* ensuring the model produces a varied set of plausible trajectories rather than converging to a single prediction.
  3. Smoothness Loss — enforces approximate physical plausibility by encouraging trajectories that are temporally smooth and consistent with realistic motion dynamics.

IV. Results

In this section, we display both quantitative results to benchmark the performance of our model, as well as qualitative trajectory predictions to explore strengths, biases, and areas of improvement for the model. All results are evaluated using a held out portion of the Waymo Dataset.

Qualitative

We first evaluated model performance through qualitative analysis on sample videos. Below, we visualize ATP's most likely predicted trajectory for a vehicle approaching an intersection:

ATM tracks the movement of an incoming car, and over 8 iterations it refines its output to predict the car will continue to go straight..

As demonstrated above, ATM, due to its training on the Waymo dataset, accurately predicts the likely movements of vehicles in a variety of scenarios. The example also highlights ATM's ability to incorporate new information in real time. As the car above approaches the intersection, the model initially predicts a right turn; however, once the car proceeds through the intersection without signaling, the model correctly updates its prediction to straight ahead. The first frame further illustrates ATM's anytime refinement in action. Even early refinement steps correctly predicted the vehicle going straight. Despite being relatively inaccurate, this is already useful in a time-constrained pipeline, but with additional computation, ATM's prediction converges closer to the ground truth.

Quantitaive

We additionally recorded quantitative results to numerically verify the effectiveness of ATP. Our evaluation focused on three key properties:

  1. Minimum Average Displacement Error(MinADE) — What is the smallest mean error between our predicted trajectories and the ground truth?
  2. Maximum Average Displacement Error(MaxADE) — What is the maximum mean error between our predicted trajectories and the ground truth? Ideally, this value should be low, similar to MinADE, yet not exactly the same. If this value is too close to MinADE, that suggests mode collapse, where our model's modes are always nearly identical
  3. Negative Log Likelihood(NLL) — How likely is the ground truth given the probability density function our model outputs(lower is better).

MaxADE 0.2135 0.2050 0.1965
NLL -68.58 -71.88 -72.45
These quantitative results provide valuable insight into our model’s behavior. As shown above, increasing the number of refinement steps does not lead to substantial improvements in best-mode accuracy. In fact, the mode closest to the ground truth remains almost unchanged across iterations. However, the other two values, NLL and MaxADE do show more improvement. The reduction in MaxADE indicates that the model’s worst predictions move closer to the ground truth with each refinement step. Similarly, the decrease in NLL suggests that the model becomes increasingly confident in the correct prediction as the number of refinement steps increases. What does this tell us? Rather than significantly improving raw accuracy, additional refinement steps primarily enhance the model’s confidence in the correct prediction. Although this differs somewhat from our original objective, it is still an important and useful result. It demonstrates that additional refinement steps help generate more reliable and informative predictions. Even if MinADE does not improve, the model’s increased ability to identify and assign higher confidence to the most likely mode is arguably just as important.

V. Conclusion and Limitations

In this work, we introduced ATP, an anytime prediction trajectory prediction model that could predict the trajectory of objects based on RGB video input with varying latency times. Qualitatively, we prove that our model is capable of predicting feasable trajectories of moving objects, quantiatively however, our model appears to not benefit from the iterative refinement process that makes up the core of our anytime-algorithm. Despite this, or model did show one unexpected result, rather than improving its accuracy with each iterative refinement step, it increased its confidence in the correct prediction. This feature, while not the original intended goal, does showcase the viability of our approach. It also suggests that with additional tuning, it may be possible to further refine our model to sequentially refine its predictions to generate more accurate trajectories as time goes on rather than simply becoming more confident in the correct trajectory.

Outside of the above note, limitations that we would like to address in future works are 2 fold: 1. addressing mode collapse, 2. Expanding to additional scenarios. As to the first point, it was noticed that our model would often tend to collapse to having all modes be extremeley close to eachother. While there are cases where this is intended, it does decrease the usefulness of our model by disregarding alternate movements of objects. Secondly, we would like to adjust our model to work in areas outside of autonomous driving. Due to the fact we trained on the Waymo dataset, our model is only capable of predicting the trajectories of cars, bikes, and pedestrians. If we wanted to expand our model to work in areas such as UAVs, we could finetune this on various scenarios as well as under different conditions to obtain various versions of or model for different environments.
